# Squad simulation: Bocage, June 1944 (and the Ardennes, December 1944)

A fork of the [village simulation](../village/README.md) turned to squad-level infantry combat. A US rifle platoon attacks a German infantry Zug dug in among the hedgerows of Normandy, one second and one soldier at a time. The village's daily life is gone: no farming, crime, courts, romance or culture. What carries over is the engine underneath: the seeded RNG streams, the event log and its hash, decision sources with replay, player interventions, one man per tile, and the health system. Wounds are tracked per body part, bleed, get dressed, and heal well or badly.

## What happens in a battle

- **The ground.** About 430 m by 650 m of Normandy (one tile is 3 m). Fields of pasture, wheat, orchard and wood are boxed in by hedgerows, which are earthen banks under thick brush. A stream crosses the south in a shallow valley, with wet meadow along its banks. The seed picks one of five kinds of place:
  - **a bocage hamlet**: small fields, a sunken lane, and a farmhouse, barn, cottage and chapel at a crossroads behind a farmyard wall;
  - **open farmland on a ridge**: big fields with brambles along the hedges, a walled farm, outlying barns, and the defended hedge along the crest;
  - **a highway junction**: a wide straight road with ditches and rows of poplars, houses, a café and a garage at the crossing, and a railway embankment across the fields;
  - **a market town**: streets on a grid around a square, terraced houses two and three storeys high, shops, a church with a bell tower, and garden walls;
  - **a shell-torn city**: no fields at all, street after street of terraced houses, shops and apartment blocks up to four storeys, many breached or fallen in. Each row of blocks has its side streets offset from the next, so streets end in T-junctions a gun in an upper window can sweep. The streets are full of rubble, shell holes, potholes, burnt-out vehicles at the kerb and debris thrown out of the houses; alleys run through the blocks between walled back yards; there are open-air markets and small plazas with fountains and statues. The defenders hold a barricaded boulevard (sandbags across every street mouth, wire in front) with machine guns in the high windows and the church tower behind it, and the town hall on its plaza, with a fountain and statues, beyond. The attackers form up in a park on the southern edge.
- **Height.** The ground rises and falls. Sight lines run from a man's eyes to his target's body, so a crest between them blocks sight, and a man on a rise or upstairs sees over hedgerows that hide him on the flat. Walking uphill costs more, a shooter above his target hits a little more often, and fire from above comes in over the bank or wall in front of a man.
- **Buildings.** Houses have rooms (inner walls with doorways), and many have upper floors reached by stairs. A church's bell tower climbs two tall storeys to a belfry with louvred openings high above the roofs: the best view on the map, and the favourite perch of snipers and machine gunners. German defenders often put a machine gun crew up there when it overlooks the enemy's way in. Men fight from upstairs windows, and a grenade lobbed through an upper window, or a shell through a roof, bursts on that floor, while floors shield the men below.
- **Weather.** Each seed brings its own: clear, overcast, rain or fog. Rain and fog cut how far men can see and spot and how far shots are heard; rain turns fields to mud, which slows men and tires them; both spoil aim a little.
- **Winter.** About one seed in three is fought in the Ardennes on 19 December 1944 instead of Normandy in June: any of the five kinds of place, deep in snow. The forest has taken more of the fields (and there is no standing wheat), and the defence has been dug in for weeks: more trenches and strongpoints behind the hedge, a foxhole every few paces along it, old holes all through the woods. The short grey days cut how far men can see even when it is clear. Snow falls on many of those mornings and cuts it further, and there is freezing fog. Deep snow drags at every step off the roads, makes the roads themselves slower, and tires men faster. Summer seeds are the battles they always were, because the season comes from its own stream of the seed.
- **The forest line.** In winter the seed can also give a sixth kind of ground, fought the way the Ardennes and the Hürtgen were: no fields and no village, only fir and beech forest over rocky ridges, a logging track and a forest road crossing at the objective, a forester's lodge there and a woodcutter's hut or two. Each side holds a trench line across the whole width of the map. The defenders' line is behind a belt of wire, with log-covered machine-gun bunkers and communication trenches back to a reserve trench at the crossroads; the attackers' line is in front of where they form up. Between them lies no man's land: forest smashed by months of shelling into stumps, deadfall and craters, stands of trees still upright, boulders, and firebreaks that make open lanes across it. Boulders are solid cover and nobody walks through them.
- **Roads.** On every map the roads have been wired and sandbagged: chicanes of sandbags that leave one lane through, coils of barbed wire on the verges, a roadblock where the road crosses the defended hedge, sandbagged posts at the ways into the settlement and the north end of the bridge. The roads are a mess, too: potholes, shell holes, debris, and carts and lorries abandoned at the verge. Sandbags are chest-high cover; wire gives none, is slow to pick through and now and then snags a man fast, standing; shells blow both apart.
- **Hedges and cover.** A hedge blocks sight and stops bullets, but a man lying right against one can see and shoot through it, and is very hard to spot until he fires.
- **The forces.** Usually the Americans attack: a platoon HQ (lieutenant, platoon sergeant, radio operator and aidman) and two or three 12-man rifle squads. Each squad has a squad leader with a Thompson, an assistant squad leader, two scouts, a BAR team (gunner, assistant and ammunition bearer), a rifle grenadier and riflemen. The defenders are a German Zugtrupp and two 10-man Gruppen, each built around an MG 42 with its three-man crew, a Truppführer, riflemen with Kar98ks, and sometimes a scoped sniper. Half the time a US sniper works with the American platoon. One battle in four is a German counter-attack on an American-held position. Most of the time the American platoon has a machine-gun team attached from the weapons platoon, attacking or defending: a sergeant, a gunner, his assistant and two ammunition bearers with a Browning M1919A4 .30, or one time in three the M2 .50. The gun has to be set up on its tripod before it fires (ten seconds for the .30, twenty for the .50, after every move), its assistant feeds the belt, and the gunner sitting up behind it while it fires is an easy target. It is the best suppressor on the field, and the .50 goes through most cover. Attacking, the team follows the rifle squads up; once the enemy is found it carries the gun to a spot with a line of fire, some cover and some height, sets up and pours fire on the position while the squads move, and moves on if it has had nothing to shoot at for a while. Defending, it is dug in on the line. Either way, enemy coming in close makes the crew pick up the gun and get out.
- **Squad tactics.** Squads advance by bounds, field by field: one team moves to the next hedge while the other watches over it, then it comes up. On contact everyone gets down and returns fire. The squad leader then decides: rush a light position, or fix the enemy with the base-of-fire team (the BAR team or MG crew) while the rifle team works round a flank along the least exposed route. When the flank is reached the rifle team assaults with grenades and rushes, the base of fire lifts and shifts as it closes, and the position is cleared. A squad that loses too many men falls back by bounds under covering fire and smoke. If one squad is stuck, the platoon leader sends another round to help, and his radio operator calls in mortar fire, unless friendly troops are too close to the target.
- **Calling for help.** A squad that is pinned down, stuck, taking losses or outnumbered calls the platoon leader: an American squad leader on his SCR-536 handie-talkie at once, a German Gruppenführer by sending a runner, which takes a while. The platoon leader sends the nearest squad that is free: attackers swing round to hit the enemy from the flank, defenders move up beside the caller and hold with them. If nobody can be spared, the mortars are told where they are wanted. A German runner is one of the squad's own men, a rifleman or the ammunition bearer, who runs to the Zugführer under whatever fire there is: he can be shot down and lies where he falls, and he can be clicked like anyone else.
- **Fighting in a city.** Men move from cover to cover: routes keep to walls and run through the houses, and a man who dashes across open street stops in the cover he reaches before the next dash. Terraced houses have doorways knocked through their party walls, on every floor, so a squad can work along a block without going into the street. A squad bounds house to house: the covering team opens up on the windows the enemy has been seen at, and only once it has them busy does the other team rush the next building, clear it room by room and take its windows on every floor, guns at the top; then the others come up, and the house is held a while before the next bound. Smoke goes into a street before a team crosses it. On contact a squad goes into the nearest building rather than lying out in the street; an enemy far up the street is not chased, the squad keeps working forward under covering fire. Within about 125 m, a machine gun or sniper in an upper window or the bell tower is pinned by the base of fire while the rifle team works round through the houses to dead ground under the window, then stacks on the walls and grenades it, through the window, up the stairs and room to room. Rifle grenades and rockets go through upper windows. Defenders whose house is being broken into go up the stairs to hold the upper floors and drop grenades down the stairwell; squads pushed back a second time fall back to the town hall, which one squad garrisons from the start and which has to be cleared to win. City attackers bring an extra rifle squad and three more mortar missions, and the defenders an anti-tank gun half as often; with that, city battles go either way about evenly, and cost both sides dear.
- **Clearing houses.** When the enemy is in a house, the assault becomes a drill. The base of fire keeps the windows busy while the assault team runs up and flattens itself against the walls beside the doors, windows and breaches. On the word every man posts a grenade through the nearest opening (lobbed up through an upstairs window if that is where the enemy is), and as they burst the team goes in, room by room and up the stairs. Defenders upstairs drop grenades on men stacked below.
- **Ambushes and fire control.** Sometimes one defending squad goes out in front of the line to lie in a wood, scrub or a hedge bank beside the way the enemy must come. They lie flat and hold their fire. So does the whole defending platoon. No gun on the line or in the ambush opens up until the killing ground (about 85 m round the ambush party, 100 m in front of the line) holds as many of the enemy as it is going to get: most of the men seen so far, or as many as came in before the count stopped growing. It also opens if an enemy is about to step on a hidden man (12 m from the ambush, 40 m from the line), or if an enemy who can see one of them shoots at him. Rounds fired at a hedge on spec, a far-off sniper or a stray mortar bomb do not give them away. Then the whole platoon opens at once: rifles and machine guns, a volley of grenades from every man in reach, and a mortar mission on a target registered before the battle. That mission comes straight down as fire for effect, with no ranging round, on the ground just behind the killing ground where the squads following up are; the defenders keep it back for this, on top of their others. Men caught in the killing ground by every gun at once are stunned, and go flat before the first of them can answer. On the word, every man fires at once, the gunners first and hardest: for the first seconds firing outranks diving for cover, men who cannot see an enemy themselves sweep the places their squad has them, and the guns, laid beforehand, shoot straighter. The ambush party then fights it out where it lies for a couple of minutes, though the enemy is close and on every side, unless it is badly hurt. In a city the party splits between two houses beside the street the enemy will come along, each fire team at the windows on one, upstairs first, so the street is under fire from two places at once. The viewer shows the countdown to the word over each ambush party in the wide view (see Viewer).
- **Destruction.** Every stretch of house and garden wall takes a few hits before it is a breach of rubble men can climb through, and roofs show every bomb that came through them. A man upstairs in a doorway that is blown out falls with it. A house shelled hard enough falls in: the roof and upper floors come down, men upstairs fall with them and men inside may be crushed, and what is left is a ruin. Shells smash woods and orchards into deadfall (stumps and splintered trunks, slow going but some cover) and blow hedgerows open. Only the tiles near each burst are touched, so this stays cheap.
- **Defence.** Defenders dig foxholes along the hedge line, with machine guns in the field corners that give the best view over the ground in front. They hold fire until the platoon gives the word (see above). When outflanked or badly hurt they fall back by bounds to the settlement's windows, upstairs first, and walls, and when too few remain they pull out. Machine gunners and automatic riflemen take the high, open-fronted spots in any position their team takes up.
- **Snipers.** A sniper works alone. He picks a hide as high and hidden as he can find, with a long view over the enemy: an upper window, the bell tower, a wood on a rise, a hedge corner. He waits for the shot that matters (the enemy sniper, a machine gunner, an officer or radioman) and he watches wounded men to kill the medic or comrade who comes for them. After a few shots, or once he is fired on, he moves. A squad he fires on yells "Sniper!", goes to ground, searches hard where they think he is, and puts fire there; their own sniper turns to hunt him.
- **Seeing and being seen.** Men in woods, brambles, dark rooms and upstairs windows are hard to spot. Firing gives a man away, but much less from cover, and a sniper's shot almost not at all: his crack gives a bearing only up close, and a man fired on by someone he cannot see judges where it came from only roughly. Mortars are only called down on positions someone has actually seen. Close in it is different: within about 40 m a man is spotted within a second or two, in the trees or flat on the ground, and a man a few paces off is hard to miss, whatever his posture, unless he is behind something solid.
- **Under mortar fire.** Men in holes and houses stay down; anyone in the open dives for a crater, ditch or wall. A squad caught in the open gets out of the beaten zone, sideways or back, and a ranging round landing close is warning enough to move before the fire for effect. Attackers already close to the enemy go forward through it instead. Rounds take a while to arrive (about 9 seconds for a mortar bomb, 12 for a shell), and in the last moments the men near where it will land hear it coming, shout "Incoming!" and hit the dirt: veterans nearly always, green men often too late.
- **Fatigue.** Running, climbing and carrying tire men out, and mud makes it worse. A blown man moves slower, aims worse, cannot rush, and stops to catch his breath when he can.
- **The start.** The attackers form up far enough back that nobody on either side can see anybody, not even a sniper through his scope, ambush parties included. They march up in column, each squad in its own lane so they come into view spread across the front, and shake out into bounds once they come within sight of the enemy's hedge.
- **Range.** In clear weather a man can make out the enemy at up to about 200 m, less in rain and fog, and a sniper with his scope out to about 350 m. Defenders hold their fire until the enemy is within about 100 m. Aimed fire is deadly close in and falls away quickly with range: at 200 to 300 m rifles and machine guns mostly suppress, though a round still finds someone now and then. A scoped rifle keeps its accuracy out to about 450 m.
- **Fire and suppression.** Most rounds miss. Each round can hit, with odds set by range, the shooter's skill, wounds and nerve, and the target's posture, cover and movement. Rounds that miss and strike a house pock its walls with bullet holes. Every round that lands near a man suppresses him. Suppressed men go prone, shoot badly and get pinned. Machine guns suppress hardest, need an assistant to feed the belt, and overheat. Gunners reload, ammunition bearers bring belts forward, and when a gunner falls his assistant takes over the gun. Rounds go through what they have the punch for: a rifle or machine-gun round through a door, a window, a log wall or a tree, and now and then through the earth and brush of a hedgerow bank; a submachine gun or a pistol barely through a door; the .50 through nearly all of it. Stone walls and houses stop everything but the .50, and that only just. What comes through hits less often, only close in, and wounds less. Men know it: an enemy known to be behind a hedge or a cabin wall gets fire put through it, the machine guns most of all.
- **Fear.** A man under fire in the open, in a field or a road, does not lie down where he is: lying flat in the open is no cover. He runs for a wall, a bank, a hole or a house, as far as a dozen paces or more if that is where the nearest is, and the more frightened he is the harder he runs. Only a man pinned flat stays put.
- **Morale.** Seeing comrades fall, losing a leader and lying under fire all wear morale down. Leaders nearby restore it and can rally pinned men. When morale breaks, a man runs for the rear, or surrenders if the enemy is close and no friend is near. Prisoners are walked back.
- **Wounds (kept from the village).** A bullet or fragment hits a body part: through the torso, the face, a shattered leg, a lost eye. Hits on men behind a hedge or wall land on whatever shows above it. Open wounds bleed, which is what kills most men, minutes after the hit, unless the medic gets to them. The aidman crawls out to the wounded, stops the bleeding and gives morphine. Any soldier can put a field dressing on a buddy, and the platoon sergeant carries out the men who cannot walk. A man who cannot walk, has passed out, is in too much pain or cannot hold his rifle is out of action. Once the fighting stops, the wounded are followed for thirty days under the same healing rules: some are back with the platoon in a week, some die of festering wounds, and some are crippled for life.
- **The end.** The attackers win if they hold the objective (the crossroads, the farm, the road junction or the town square) unopposed, or if the defenders are destroyed or pull out. The defenders win if the attack breaks. The wounded the losers leave behind become prisoners. After seventy minutes with neither (an hour's fighting, and the march up), the attack has stalled.

Every run is reproducible from its seed, its decision log and its intervention log.

## Running

Node.js 22.18 or later. `npm install` only adds dev tools.

```bash
npm install
npm run simulate -- --seed 42
```

```
Squad simulation, seed 42: 12 June 1944, the US platoon attacks (519 ticks, to 06:48:39; 3304 ms)
  ground    a bocage hamlet, a clear, bright morning; snipers: us, de
  outcome   US victory: the defenders were killed, captured or driven off
  US        41 men: 1 killed, 19 wounded, 0 captured, 0 left the field; 425 rounds, 22 hits
  German    24 men: 4 killed, 18 wounded, 17 captured, 3 left the field; 414 rounds, 19 hits
  ordnance  38 grenades thrown, 14 mortar and artillery rounds
  medics    6 treatments; a month on, 11 more dead of wounds, 5 never to fight again
```

Add `--map urban` (or `hamlet`, `rural`, `road`, `town`) to choose the ground instead of letting the seed pick it, and `--season winter` (or `summer`) to choose the season. Add `--out runs/seed42` to write `events.ndjson`, `decisions.ndjson`, `interventions.ndjson` and `summary.json`, and `npm run replay -- --dir runs/seed42` to check that the run reproduces exactly. `--ticks N` stops after N seconds instead of at the end of the battle.

## Viewer

```bash
npm run viewer
```

Then open http://localhost:5174 (add `?seed=7` for another battle, `&map=urban` for a city, `&map=forest` for the forest line, and `&season=winter` for the snow). The featured and daily seeds were picked as summer battles and stay summer unless `season` says otherwise.

In winter the ground lies under snow, trodden to slush on the roads and iced at the edge of the stream. Trees and hedges are heaped with it, roofs are white, and the minimap turns to snowfields and dark forest. The men wear greatcoats: the Americans the long olive drab overcoat with a knitted scarf, gloves and overshoes, the Germans the double-breasted field-grey Mantel with a wool toque. There is snow on every helmet, and about one in three is daubed white. Falling snow drifts down over the battle.

In the wide view an ambush party shows a box over it, "AMBUSH · FIRE IN 0:12", counting down to when the platoon reckons it will give the word, with how many of the enemy are in the killing ground against how many it is waiting for; the killing ground itself is a faint dashed ring. The box flashes FIRE! when the word is given.

The machine guns sound like their guns and fire at their own rate: the MG 42 rips at 1,200 rounds a minute, the BAR and the Brownings thud along at about 500. The Brownings have no recordings of their own in the bank: the .30 is the BAR recording pitched down and weighted in the low end, and the .50 is the MG 42 recording pitched down by a third with a heavy low end and some saturation.

- Drag or use **WASD** to pan, and the wheel or **Q/E** to zoom: in big strides when zoomed out over the whole field, in fine steps when close in. Space pauses, and keys 1 to 6 set the speed from 1x (real time) to 40x.
- Click a soldier, or a name in the **Men** tab, to see his portrait (helmet, wounds and all), a body diagram coloured by wound, what he is doing, his health, morale, suppression, pain and bleeding, his weapon and ammunition, his character, his wounds and how they were dressed, and what has happened to him. **F** follows him.
- **Squads** shows each squad's state and plan in words, for example: "machine gun at 87 m: base of fire pins it, right flank for the rifle team". It also lists what each fire team is ordered to do, who leads, and who is still on his feet.
- **Events** and the log overlay tell the story: contacts, orders, shouted callouts, hits, the medic's work, fire missions, surrenders. **Report** opens the after-action report, which also opens by itself when the battle ends. It lists losses on each side, notable deeds, every man killed, and every wounded man's fate a month later.
- Overlays: **Orders** (O) draws where each man has been sent, the positions being suppressed (circles), enemy positions (red crosses) and flanking objectives. **Sight** (V) shows who the selected man can see, **Names** (N) labels everyone, and **Roofs** (R) hides the roofs. Hover a house to see inside.
- Zoomed in, each house has a small floor button by its roof: click it to cut the house away floor by floor (ground floor, first floor, second floor, then the roof again) and see its rooms, stairs and the men on that floor. Selecting a man indoors shows his floor.
- The ground steps down in shaded terraces wherever it falls away, so slopes and crests read at a glance. Under the minimap, which has a lettered grid, the grid square, tile, height and ground under the pointer are shown. Buildings stand in three-quarter view with a storey of windows per floor, and show the bullet holes they have taken. Rain and grey skies lie over the battle, and fog lies on the ground itself, pooling in the hollows and drifting round the men's legs while the houses stand out of it. The alerts box reports squads making contact, flanking, assaulting, falling back, retreating, getting out from under mortar fire, calling for help and being sent it, springing ambushes and clearing houses, and houses falling in; click a squad's alert to take the camera to that squad. Walls and trees knocked down throw up their pieces, and a collapsing house a cloud of dust.
- Powers: **💥 Barrage** (X) brings down six 105 mm shells, **💨 Smoke** (Z) lays a smoke screen, and **🎯 Sniper** (K) has someone shot by an unseen marksman. All three are logged and replay exactly.
- **Orders.** Zoomed out, click a squad's label (or **Orders** in the Squads tab) for its orders, beside the powers: **Move to**, **Assault** and **Suppress** take a click on the map, **Hold** keeps the squad where it is, and **Carry on** gives it back to the platoon's plan. The squad carries out the order itself: it bounds to the place and holds it; covers the assault with its base of fire and clears houses with grenades; finds lines of fire on what it is told to suppress. The label reads "Under your orders", and a dashed line runs to the marked spot.
- **Taking control.** Select a man and press **T** (or **Take control**). WASD moves him, Shift runs, C stands, crouches or lies him down, he faces the pointer, a click fires there, G or a right-click throws a grenade, R reloads and T lets him go. He keeps his own ammunition, is wounded like anyone else, and is lost to you if he is killed or captured. On a phone, a thumb stick moves him and a touch on the map fires. The speed drops to 1x.

In the browser console, `squad.advance(60)` runs a minute, `squad.look(x, y, zoom)` moves the camera, `squad.order(squadId, "move", x, y)` gives an order and `squad.control(id)` takes a man (`squad.control(null)` lets him go).

## First visit, cinematic mode and analytics

- **Cinematic start.** A first visit has no start gate. The march up is run unseen to the first shots, the camera glides to the busiest visible fight (it never cuts, and any drag, key or zoom takes it back), and the screen shows only the clock, pause, sound, Auto-cam, **Take full control**, and one big **Call in a barrage** button that lands on the nearest enemy in view. Alerts about a man or a place can be tapped to look at them. Sound starts on the first tap, as browsers require.
- **Later battles.** From the second battle on, a chooser offers **Jump to the action** or **Watch from the start**, with a tick box to turn the cinematic features off for good (`localStorage` key `squad.cinematic`; the Auto-cam button turns them back on). `?start=1` or `?cinematic=0` opens a link without them. Without `?seed=` a first visit gets one of the seeds in `FEATURED_SEEDS` in `viewer/src/main.ts`, chosen because the first contact is in the open.
- **Share.** A finished battle's report has **Share this result**: a 1200 x 630 card (verdict, losses, seed) through the system share sheet, or saved as a PNG with the replay link copied.
- **Analytics.** `viewer/src/analytics.ts` records anonymous counters (time to first interaction, speed changes, barrages, battle end, a 15 second heartbeat and a last event on leaving). On sim.shaykas.com the page sets `window.SIM_ANALYTICS = "/a/e"`; nginx proxies that to `analytics/collect.ts`, which appends anonymous lines (no IP, cookie or user agent string is stored) to `/var/lib/sim-analytics/events.ndjson`. `village/deploy/analytics-setup.sh` installs it, and `sudo node --no-warnings analytics/report.ts` prints the funnel. Elsewhere nothing is sent (set `window.SIM_ANALYTICS` to any JSON endpoint, or load Plausible); the last 60 events sit in `sessionStorage` under `squad.events`.

## Layout

```
src/
  engine/      tick loop and activities, RNG streams, invariants, player interventions
  world/       types, the map (height, floors, sight, cover, paths), the map generator for each kind of ground (city.ts for the city), road defences, the world
  entities/    body.ts and injuries.ts (the village's wound system), death, morale and suppression
  combat/      small-arms fire, grenades/mortars/smoke, what shells knock down, spotting and contact reports
  ai/          command (platoon and squad leaders), each soldier's options, tactical geometry, snipers, ambushes
  decision/    DecisionSource interface, utility scorer, replay
  battle/      the thirty days after
  scenario/    config (every tunable number), force generation
  cli/         simulate and replay commands
test/
viewer/        pixel-art browser viewer (build.ts bundles it with esbuild, serve.ts serves it)
  audio/       the lo-fi sound bank (bank.bin + bank.json), built by sfx/build.py
```

## Sound

The viewer plays gunshots, reloads, footsteps by ground type, explosions, shell whistles, building collapses, voices and weather beds, placed by distance and pan from the middle of the screen. Sounds made indoors ring off the walls: a house gets a short room reverb, a church, barn or warehouse a long booming one (synthesised impulse responses, no extra files); a house that has fallen in has no roof to ring. `M` mutes. The sounds are packed in `viewer/audio/`; to rebuild them from the source recordings:

```
pip install numpy scipy
python sfx/build.py "<Raw Master Tracks>" "<Essentials_Series_NOX_SOUND>"
```

Gunshot recordings carry 10-35 s of faint room echo; each is cut at the point the tail actually dies (at most 1.7 s) and faded. Everything is band-limited, saturated and bit-crushed at 16 kHz for the lo-fi sound. The sheet of what each take is, and which take fed which sound, is in `viewer/audio/sources.json`.

The choices made along the way are in [`DECISIONS.md`](DECISIONS.md).
